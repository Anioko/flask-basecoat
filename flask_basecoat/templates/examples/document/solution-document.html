{{ section_title('Executive summary') }}

Harbourline settles about 410,000 claims a year on a claims platform that went live in 2009. It runs on hardware the manufacturer stopped supporting in 2024, it cannot price the parametric home product the board approved for 2027, and four of the six people who understand its batch scheduler have left or will retire within two years. This document recommends how to replace it.

We compared four options against five weighted criteria (Table 1). Option C, replacing the platform with a packaged claims product and moving one line of business at a time, scores highest at 76.3 out of 100. It costs £11.0m over three years, within £0.2m of keeping the current system running, and it is the only option that meets the product timetable without leaving the group dependent on a handful of specialists.

The main risk is delivery capacity, not technology. On today's team the forecast reaches 90 per cent of claims migrated by the end of 2028, not 100 (Figure 2). The recommendation therefore includes four additional integration engineers from the third quarter of 2027. The remaining sections set out the context, the options and how we scored them, the cost and delivery picture, the risks, and the next six steps.

{{ section_title('Context and objectives') }}

The platform handles registration, reserving, payments and recovery for motor, home and commercial claims. It was built for a book one third of today's size. Peak-season handling times have risen from 11 to 17 days in three years, and 38 per cent of handler time is spent re-keying data between the platform and the document system.

What the programme must achieve

The executive committee set three objectives in July 2026. First, support the 2027 home product, which needs claims to be triggered by weather data without a handler opening a case. Second, cut the average handling time for a standard motor claim from 17 days to 9. Third, remove the single points of failure: no component should depend on one person, and the recovery time objective for claims should fall from 36 hours to 4.

Constraints we accepted

Claims data stays in the United Kingdom. The regulator's outsourcing rules apply to any option that moves processing to a supplier, including the right to audit and a tested exit plan. Customers must not see a change in service levels during migration, and the programme may not run beyond the end of 2028 because the current hardware support contract ends that December.

What we did

Between August and October 2026 the architecture team interviewed 23 handlers, team leaders and managers, reviewed twelve months of incident and change records, profiled the motor and home data, and held working sessions with four suppliers and with the group technology platform team. Costs are three-year estimates in 2026 pounds, reviewed by finance.

{{ section_title('Cost and evaluation') }}

Option A is cheapest to start and dearest to run: patching adds about £3.2m a year in support and workarounds by 2028. Option D is the reverse, with the lowest running cost and a build cost of £11.8m that no other benefit offsets. Options B and C sit between them. Option C costs £5.1m to implement and £5.9m to run over three years, a total of £11.0m against £10.8m for Option A (Figure 1).

Cost alone does not separate A, B and C, so the scores weigh what the money buys. Option C wins on strategic fit because the product ships parametric triggers and a handler workbench that we would otherwise build. It loses ground to A and B on delivery risk, which is why risk carries its own section below. Option D scores full marks for fit and for control of data, but its twelve-month build delays the first line of business to 2029, after the hardware support contract ends.

Sensitivity was tested by moving each weight by ten points either way. Option C stays first in every case. Option B overtakes it only if delivery risk is weighted above 53 per cent, more than two and a half times the weight the committee chose.

{{ section_title('Delivery plan') }}

Option C is delivered in three waves. Motor goes first because its data is the cleanest and it carries the largest volume: build and parallel run from April 2027, cutover in the fourth quarter. Home follows in the second quarter of 2028 with the parametric product, and commercial lines complete the migration in the last quarter of 2028, ahead of the end of hardware support.

The planned line assumes the integration team has fourteen engineers from the third quarter of 2027. Today it has ten. On that smaller team the forecast falls behind from the first wave and reaches 90 per cent at the end of 2028, ten points short of the date the hardware contract allows. The recommendation closes that gap by adding four engineers, at a cost already inside the £11.0m envelope.

Each cutover is gated. A line of business moves only after two consecutive weeks in parallel run in which payments match to the penny and service levels are at or above target. If a gate is missed the wave waits; the plan has six weeks of slack in each wave for that reason.

{{ section_title('Recommendation') }}

We recommend Option C: replace the claims platform with a packaged claims product, migrating motor, then home, then commercial lines, with the platform retired by the end of 2028.

Why this option

It is the only option that meets the product timetable, the date the hardware support ends and the ambition to cut handling time to nine days. It scores 76.3 out of 100, against 58.8 for the next option, and it holds first place under every sensitivity test we ran. It also removes the dependence on a few specialists, because the supplier carries platform operations and the group keeps only configuration and integration skills.

What we ask the committee to approve

An envelope of £11.0m over three years, of which £5.1m is one-off. Four additional integration engineers from the third quarter of 2027. Authority to negotiate with the two shortlisted suppliers, with exit assistance and data return as conditions. A programme director reporting to the chief operating officer, and a steering group that reviews every cutover gate.

What the group gets

By the end of 2029 the legacy run cost of £3.1m a year is gone, the average handling time for a standard motor claim is nine days, the recovery time objective is four hours, and the parametric home product launches without custom build. The benefit case, reviewed by finance, pays back the one-off cost in the second half of 2030.
